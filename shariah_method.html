Shariah Screening Methodology

Last updated: June 2025

A note before anything else

Shariah compliance is not private property, and there is no single, universally agreed global standard for it[cite: 2]. Different scholars, boards, and jurisdictions apply different — sometimes differing — thresholds and interpretations, each working in good faith from the same core sources[cite: 2]. Onucrom is not a Shariah board[cite: 2]. We do not issue fatwas, and we do not claim final authority over what is or isn't compliant for anyone's personal religious reliance[cite: 2].

What we do offer is a transparent, consistently-applied screening process, so you can see at a glance where something is likely to stand — and, just as importantly, see exactly why[cite: 2]. Our rating is a directional signal, not a guarantee, and not a replacement for your own scholar's guidance on a matter of personal religious obligation[cite: 2].

Two paths to a rating

Path 1 — Already certified

If a business, fund, or instrument has already been reviewed and confirmed by a recognized Shariah supervisory board or committee, we start there[cite: 2]. We don't re-run or second-guess a qualified scholar panel's own ruling[cite: 2]. To carry certified status on Onucrom, we require public disclosure of:

  • The name of the certifying authority (the Shariah board or committee)[cite: 2].
  • Where publicly available, the name of that committee's head or chair[cite: 2].

Where we can't find or confirm this information publicly, the listing is treated under Path 2 instead[cite: 2].

Path 2 — Not yet certified: Onucrom's stepped screen

Where no recognized certification exists, we apply our own four-step process[cite: 2]. This does not create a certification[cite: 2]. It produces our own estimate, clearly labeled as such, and nothing more[cite: 2].

How the assessment actually works: Asset-type lanes

The single biggest lever for keeping this to 1-2 minutes is not asking questions that don't apply[cite: 3]. The very first question routes the user into one of three lanes, and everything downstream is scoped to that lane[cite: 3].

  • Lane A — Equity / Business (listed stock, private equity, a business raising capital): the full path — Certification → Prohibited Exposure → Financial → Instrument → Niyat[cite: 3].
  • Lane B — Debt / Fixed-income instrument (bond, sukuk, treasury security): Certification → Instrument (core, since there's no "business activity" to screen the same way) → Niyat[cite: 3]. Financial ratio and business-activity questions are skipped — they don't apply to the instrument type[cite: 3].
  • Lane C — Deposit / Savings product (FDR, savings account, Mudaraba deposit): Certification → Instrument only (a short, largely binary check) → Niyat[cite: 3].

The Four-Step Screen Details

Step 1 — Prohibited Exposure Filter (Lane A only)
We look at what the business actually does[cite: 2]. Is its core activity — or a material part of it — in an area classical scholarship treats as impermissible (for example: conventional banking or insurance, alcohol, gambling, and similar)?[cite: 2] Where such exposure exists but is minor or incidental rather than central to the business, we record the extent of it rather than applying an automatic fail[cite: 2]. Presence and materiality are both part of the picture — not presence alone[cite: 2].
Step 2 — Financial Filter (Lane A only)
For businesses with disclosed financials, we apply ratio-based screening drawing primarily on AAOIFI standards[cite: 2]. Alongside the standard ratios, we ask one additional question: are the business's primary banking relationships with Islamic banks?[cite: 2] This isn't a hard ratio threshold — it's a signal of the business's own orientation, treated as a useful supporting input, not a standalone pass/fail[cite: 2].
Step 3 — Instrument Filter (All lanes)
Separately from the business itself, we examine the investment vehicle[cite: 2]. Certain instrument features make an otherwise-acceptable business's securities non-compliant regardless of what the business does — for example, a guaranteed fixed return or an interest-bearing structure[cite: 2]. These disqualifying features are checked explicitly at this stage[cite: 2].
Step 4 — Niyat (Intention) Filter (All lanes)
This step is about you, not the instrument[cite: 2]. We ask a short question about your intention going in: are you approaching this as genuine holding/participation, or primarily chasing short-term trading gains?[cite: 2] Your answer never changes the instrument's rating[cite: 2]. What it does is generate a separate comment if your stated approach leans toward a pattern Shariah scholarship generally discourages — excessive short-term speculation — as a gentle flag, not a judgment, and entirely independent of whether the instrument itself passes or fails[cite: 2].

What you get at the end

Every completed assessment returns:

  • A color rating — 🟢 Green (compliant), 🔵 Blue (appears aligned, not certified), ⚪ Grey (unclear / insufficient information), 🟠 Orange (appears not aligned), or 🔴 Red (non-compliant)[cite: 2].
  • Flagged concerns, where relevant — for any non-Green result, we name the specific issue(s) behind it: presence of riba, gharar, maysir, or a haram business/subject-matter exposure — so you see why, not just what[cite: 2].
  • The niyat comment, if applicable, shown separately from the rating itself[cite: 2].
  • A validity note — A rating reflects the information available at the time it was generated, not a permanent judgment[cite: 2]. Financial ratios shift, business activities change, certifications get renewed or withdrawn[cite: 2]. Every rating carries a review/validity marker, and a rating past its review window is shown as needs re-review, not silently left as-is[cite: 2].

Where this methodology draws from

This isn't built from nothing[cite: 2]. It draws on established Islamic finance screening practice, including AAOIFI Shariah Standards, and the general structure used by recognized index providers (for example, the S&P/Dow Jones Islamic Market family and the methodology behind DSE's own Shariah index)[cite: 2]. Where we've added our own layer — the niyat filter, the extent-based treatment of minor prohibited exposure, and the Islamic-banking-relationship question — we say so explicitly here, rather than presenting it as drawn from an external standard it isn't[cite: 2].
